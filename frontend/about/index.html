What this is

A map of current world events, built automatically from public data feeds and news headlines, so anyone can see what is being reported, where, and by whom.

It is not a news outlet. It writes no articles and publishes no opinions. Every item points to the organisation that reported it, and the original is one click away. There are no accounts, no ads and no cookies, and the code is open source.

Honesty rules

  • Always the source. Every item names who reported it and links to the original.
  • Headlines are never rewritten. You read them as the outlet published them. Only the outlet's own name is removed from the end of a page title (e.g. " | The Guardian").
  • Duplicates are grouped. When several outlets report one story, it is one event listing all of them.
  • Many regions and viewpoints. Outlets from every region are included, also state-affiliated ones, which carry a visible label. All of them are listed on the Sources page.
  • Data and news are told apart. Every item says whether it is a "Data feed" (measured or issued by a scientific or official body) or a "News report".
  • No article text is copied. News items show only the headline, the outlet, the time and a link.
  • You can see how fresh it is. The site shows when each feed last updated, and says so when the data may be out of date or a feed has failed.
  • Rules, not guesses. Severity and categories come from published rules, and each card says why an event got its level.

How it works

  1. Collect. Every 30 minutes an automatic run fetches earthquakes from USGS, disaster alerts from GDACS, and, through the GDELT Project, what the listed outlets have published.
  2. Apply the rules. Each item gets a place, categories and a severity, and reports of the same story are grouped.
  3. Publish. The result is saved as plain files and shown on the globe. The last 72 hours load first, and the timeline reaches back 7 days.

How severity is decided

Every event has one of four levels. They differ in colour, height and pulse, so colour is never the only signal, and the colours are chosen to stay distinct with colour blindness.

  • Earthquakes. USGS's impact alert (PAGER, its estimate of the effect on people) decides when there is one. Otherwise the magnitude does.
  • News. Only the headline is read, because it is what you see and the rule can be checked against it. Listed dangers include air strikes, shelling, invasions, coups, explosions, hostages, massacres, evacuations, martial law, curfews, states of emergency, outbreaks, epidemics, pandemics, famine and tsunami warnings.
  • Several reports of one story. The event takes the highest level any of its sources gives.
  • What never raises severity: how many outlets cover a story. Prominence is not severity.

On the globe, news is a pillar of light and a natural disaster is a symbol showing its type. Events close together merge into a group with a number, which takes the level of its most severe event. The full rules: severity rules on GitHub.

How categories are decided

There are eleven categories: War, Politics, Natural disasters, Economic, Technology advancements, Health & outbreaks, Climate & environment, Crime & justice, Humanitarian & migration, Science & space, and Sport. An item can have more than one.

  1. Data feeds (earthquakes, disaster alerts) are Natural disasters.
  2. Articles from an outlet's sport section are Sport, and only Sport.
  3. For other news, the outlet's own section (when its web address shows one) and listed words in the headline decide together.
  4. If those give nothing, the topics GDELT detected in the article decide.
  5. An item in none of the eleven categories is not shown.

Each card says where its category came from. The categories follow what people around the world look for in the news, and are reviewed once a year against Google's Year in Search. The full word lists: categories on GitHub.

Places and grouping

  • Data feeds give their own coordinates.
  • News is placed by software: GDELT reads each article and records the places it mentions. An item is shown only if its headline names a place, and it stands there. When the headline names only a country, the card says "country level".
  • The same story means the same country, within 24 hours, with headlines sharing most of their meaningful words. Earthquakes from USGS and GDACS are merged when they are within 30 minutes and 150 km of each other. News and data feeds are never merged.
  • Balance. Each outlet can have at most 15 news events a day, and 5 sport events counted apart, so no single outlet can fill the globe. The Sources page has the details.

Known limits

  • Most news is rated low. A headline with no death count or listed danger is low, however important the story is. This keeps the rule checkable instead of guessing what matters.
  • Places can be wrong, because they are worked out by software. Machine-coded places say so on their card.
  • Headlines can disagree on numbers. When they do, the highest level wins.
  • Coverage is not complete. Only the listed outlets appear, and only their English-language editions where an outlet publishes in several languages.
  • Do not use this site for safety decisions. Disaster alerts are automated estimates. Follow your national and local authorities.
